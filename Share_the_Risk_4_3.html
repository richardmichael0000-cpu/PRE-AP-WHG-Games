SHARE THE RISK

Merchants, voyages, and the invention of joint-stock companies
10:00CLASS GAME TIMER
READY TO PLAY
Six merchant houses · Four merchants per house · 10 coins each
Your mission: You are merchants in the early 1600s. Invest in overseas trade. Survive shipwrecks. Make a fortune.
Each merchant owns 10 coins. Each voyage costs 10 coins. Every group of four represents four merchants. The merchant letters A–D are your seats/positions.

Round 1 · Sail alone

You each pay for your OWN ship. If it sinks, you lose your entire investment.

Round 2 · Buy shares

Combine 40 coins into a COMPANY. Each merchant owns 10 of 40 shares, or 25%.

Rolls: 1–2 = shipwreck (0 returned) · 3–4 = good voyage (15 returned) · 5–6 = great voyage (25 returned). These are fictional game odds, not historical figures.

The same four voyages will be used in both rounds. Only ownership changes.

Round 1: Separate investors

Each investor owns one ship and receives only that ship's proceeds.

One shipwreck can wipe out that merchant's 10-coin investment.

Round 2: Joint-stock company

Four merchants combine 40 coins and buy 10 shares each out of 40. The company finances four voyages.

Each merchant receives 25% of the company's total proceeds, whether the overall result is profit or loss.

Ask the class

  1. Did joining a company create extra profit, or change who carried the risk?
  2. Why would this make it easier to finance large fleets and imperial expansion?
  3. Source 6: Why would the English Crown grant merchants exclusive rights to trade?
Historical connection: Early English East India Company investment often financed individual voyages; the Dutch VOC (1602) had more durable pooled capital. This is a simplified simulation. Actual investors might retain shares and receive dividends over time. Pooling reduces exposure to any single ship but never eliminates risk.
